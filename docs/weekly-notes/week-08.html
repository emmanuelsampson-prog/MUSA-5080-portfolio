Week 8: Where Is the Model Wrong? Spatial Autocorrelation in Errors

From tracts to house sales, cleaning sales data, buffer and kNN spatial features, residual maps, spatial lags, Moran’s I and permutation tests, why not a spatial lag model, and the Chicago house price prediction assignment.
Published

October 16, 2026

1. Overview

This week has three parts:

  1. From tracts to houses. We apply the same tools to individual house sales instead of census tracts.
  2. Where is the model wrong? We map the errors and measure whether they cluster in space, using Moran’s I.
  3. Assignment 4: House Price Prediction, Chicago. This is a team competition with a real, hidden test set.

The big idea:

If nearby houses have related errors, the model is missing something about place.

2. Recap: Reading a Logged Predictor

From the Week 7 lecture (Oct 9), we predicted tract rent using the log of median income:

\[ \widehat{\text{rent}} = \beta_0 + 210 \cdot \log(\text{income}) \]

Reading the coefficient (\(\hat{\beta}\))

A 1% increase in income goes with about:

\[ \frac{210}{100} = \$2.10 \]

more rent.

Reading the prediction (\(\hat{y}\))

Logging income lets the model bend. It flattens out at high incomes, so it does not over-predict rent in the richest tracts.

The rule

Log X, keep Y in dollars: β / 100 = the change in Y for a 1% change in X.

3. Recap: The Fold That Broke

In Week 7, cross-validation with planning-district fixed effects failed with this error:

Error: factor district has new levels Upper Far Northeast

What happened?

  • One small district had all of its tracts in the test fold.
  • So the model was trained without ever seeing that district.
  • It had no coefficient for it, so it could not make a prediction.

Fix

Merge small groups before cross-validation.

Why it matters this week

The same problem comes back with neighborhoods. Philadelphia has about 150 of them, and some have only a handful of sales.

4. From Tracts to Houses

The tools are the same. What changes is the unit of analysis and its shape.

Previous assignment House price prediction
Unit Census tract (polygon) Single sale (point)
n ~400 ~20,000
Count feature Crimes in the tract Crimes within 660 ft of the house
Nearest feature Distance to the nearest station Mean distance to the 5 nearest
Place feature Distance to City Hall Same

660 feet is 1/8 of a mile (5,280 ÷ 8).

Nothing here is new. A buffer is just a polygon you draw yourself.

With points, there is no tract boundary to count “inside”. So we draw our own area around each house with a buffer and count what falls in it.

The data come from Philadelphia’s Office of Property Assessment (OPA) properties table, queried through the city’s Carto SQL API.

8. Spatial Features: Buffer Count and kNN Distance

Load violent crime

#| eval: false
violent <- carto_get(
  "SELECT the_geom FROM incidents_part1_part2
   WHERE dispatch_date >= '2023-01-01' AND dispatch_date < '2025-01-01'
     AND text_general_code IN ('Aggravated Assault Firearm',
                               'Aggravated Assault No Firearm',
                               'Robbery Firearm', 'Robbery No Firearm')"
) %>%
  filter(!st_is_empty(.)) %>%
  st_transform(2272)

Violent crime is defined here as aggravated assaults and robberies, with or without a firearm, in 2023 and 2024.

Build two features

#| eval: false
sales <- sales %>%
  mutate(
    violent_660ft = lengths(st_intersects(st_buffer(., 660), violent)),
    violent_knn5  = sapply(
      st_nn(., violent, k = 5, returnDist = TRUE, progress = FALSE)$dist,
      mean
    )
  )
Feature How it is built Meaning
violent_660ft Draw a 660 ft buffer around each house, then count crimes that intersect it How many violent crimes are within 1/8 mile
violent_knn5 Find the 5 nearest crimes to each house, then average their distances How close violent crime is

How the code works:

  • st_intersects() returns, for each buffer, the list of crimes inside it. lengths() counts them.
  • st_nn() (from nngeo) finds the k nearest crimes. With returnDist = TRUE, it also returns the distances. sapply(..., mean) averages them for each house.

Which is better?

Buffer count kNN distance
Can be zero? Yes, for many houses Never
Direction More crime = higher value Smaller distance = more crime nearby

Try both, and let the cross-validated RMSE decide.

11. From “By Eye” to a Number

In the Week 5 lab and the gap assignment, we compared residual maps before and after adding features, by eye.

Today we put a number on the question:

Do the errors cluster?

Tobler’s First Law of Geography

“Near things are more related than distant things.”

Applied to model errors:

If near houses have related errors, the model is missing something about place.

When errors are random in space, the model has captured location well. When red errors sit together and blue errors sit together, something about those places is not in the model.

12. The Spatial Lag: My Neighbors’ Average

#| eval: false
coords <- st_coordinates(sales)

w_knn <- nb2listw(
  knn2nb(knearneigh(coords, k = 5)),
  style = "W"
)

sales$resid_lag <- lag.listw(w_knn, sales$resid)

Step by step (all functions from spdep):

Step Function What it does
1 st_coordinates() Get the x, y coordinates of each house
2 knearneigh(coords, k = 5) Find each house’s 5 nearest houses
3 knn2nb() Turn that into a neighbor list
4 nb2listw(..., style = "W") Turn neighbors into weights. "W" = row-standardized: each neighbor gets weight 1/5
5 lag.listw() For each house, compute the weighted average of its neighbors’ residuals

Each house gets the average residual of its 5 nearest neighbors. This is the “spatial lag”.

13. Me vs. My Neighbors (Moran Scatterplot)

#| eval: false
ggplot(sales, aes(resid, resid_lag)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.15, size = 0.6) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE, color = "#b2182b") +
  coord_cartesian(xlim = c(-3e5, 3e5), ylim = c(-2e5, 2e5)) +
  labs(x = "My residual", y = "Average of my 5 neighbors' residuals")
  • x-axis: my residual
  • y-axis: my neighbors’ average residual

If the line slopes upward, houses with high errors tend to have neighbors with high errors.

The slope of this line is Moran’s I (with row-standardized weights).

coord_cartesian() zooms in without dropping any points from the regression line.

14. Moran’s I: The “Scary” Formula

\[ I = \frac{n}{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij}} \cdot \frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij}(x_i - \bar{x})(x_j - \bar{x})} {\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \]

where:

  • \(n\) = number of observations
  • \(x_i\) = the value (here, the residual) at location \(i\)
  • \(\bar{x}\) = the mean value
  • \(w_{ij}\) = the weight between \(i\) and \(j\) (non-zero if they are neighbors)

What it is really asking

When I’m above average, are my neighbors above average too?

Breaking down the parts:

Part Meaning
\((x_i - \bar{x})(x_j - \bar{x})\) Positive if neighbors are on the same side of the mean, negative if they differ
\(\sum \sum w_{ij}(\dots)\) Add this up over all neighbor pairs
\(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2\) Divide by the total variation, to scale it
\(n / \sum \sum w_{ij}\) Adjust for the number of observations and links

15. Worked Example: Five Houses on a Block

Five houses sit in a row. Each house’s neighbors are the houses next door.

A — B — C — D — E

Step 1: Deviations from the mean

Mean residual:

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{100 + 80 + 20 - 50 - 70}{5} = \frac{80}{5} = +16\text{k} \]

House Residual − mean (16k) Deviation
A +100k − 16k +84
B +80k − 16k +64
C +20k − 16k +4
D −50k − 16k −66
E −70k − 16k −86

A positive residual means we under-predicted that house.

Step 2: Multiply each neighbor pair

Pair Product Note
A–B (+84)(+64) = +5,376 Both under-predicted
B–C (+64)(+4) = +256
C–D (+4)(−66) = −264 Different signs
D–E (−66)(−86) = +5,676 Both over-predicted
Sum 11,044

Each pair counts twice (A→B and B→A):

\[ 2 \times 11{,}044 = 22{,}088 \]

Step 3: Total variation

\[ \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 7{,}056 + 4{,}096 + 16 + 4{,}356 + 7{,}396 = 22{,}920 \]

Step 4: Put it together

  • \(n = 5\) houses
  • \(\sum \sum w_{ij} = 8\) (4 pairs × 2 directions, each with weight 1)

\[ I = \frac{5}{8} \cdot \frac{22{,}088}{22{,}920} \approx \mathbf{0.60} \]

Strong clustering: the under-predictions sit together, and so do the over-predictions.

16. Reading Moran’s I

Value of I Pattern For our errors
Near +1 Similar values cluster The model is missing something about place
Near 0 No spatial pattern The errors look like noise in space
Below 0 Neighbors differ Rare for errors. Check your weights

The goal is to push Moran’s I of the residuals toward 0 as you add features.

17. Is That Big? Shuffle and See

#| eval: false
mc <- moran.mc(sales$resid, w_knn, nsim = 499)

mc$statistic
mc$p.value
statistic
0.2605251

[1] 0.002

How the permutation test works

Compute the real Moran's I
        ↓
Shuffle the residuals randomly across houses (499 times)
        ↓
Compute Moran's I for every shuffle
        ↓
Does the real I beat almost all the shuffles?
        ↓
If yes → the clustering isn't luck

With 499 shuffles, the smallest possible p-value is:

\[ \frac{1}{499 + 1} = 0.002 \]

So \(p = 0.002\) means the real I was larger than every shuffle.

Here, I ≈ 0.26: moderate clustering of errors that is clearly not random.

18. p-Value Warning, Again

With about 20,000 houses, nearly everything is “significant”.

So the p-value tells us very little.

Watch the size of I fall as you add features. That’s the diagnostic.

19. Clustered Errors Are a Feature You Haven’t Built Yet

If Moran’s I is high, ask:

What’s true about those places that the model doesn’t know?

What’s missing Fix
An amenity or disamenity you haven’t measured (parks, transit, crime, vacancy) Build a spatial feature
Something about the whole neighborhood Add a neighborhood fixed effect
A relationship that differs by place Add an interaction

Then:

Refit the model
        ↓
Recompute CV RMSE and Moran's I
        ↓
Log both numbers

20. “My Spatial Stats Class Says Use a Spatial Lag Model”

A spatial lag model puts neighbors’ outcomes on the right-hand side:

\[ y = \rho W y + X\beta + \varepsilon \]

where \(Wy\) is the average price of neighboring houses and \(\rho\) measures how strongly it matters.

That is the right tool for inference about spillovers (for example, “does a house’s price depend on its neighbors’ prices?”).

For prediction, it fails in three ways:

  1. It needs neighbors’ prices. To price a house, you would need the prices of nearby houses that have not sold yet.
  2. It leaks. In cross-validation, a test house’s neighbors in the training set carry its answer.
  3. It explains nothing. “It’s near expensive houses” is not a reason a policymaker can act on.

Our rule

Spatial features of X, never Y.

Allowed Not allowed
Crime near the house Prices near the house
Distance to transit, parks, City Hall Neighbors’ sale prices

  • Your CV RMSE is your estimate.
  • The test RMSE is the truth.

  • Using neighbors’ prices as a feature
  • Cleaning or building features using information from the test set
  • Fixed-effect groups too small to appear in every fold

## 8. Spatial Features: Buffer Count and kNN Distance

### Load violent crime

```r
#| eval: false
violent <- carto_get(
  "SELECT the_geom FROM incidents_part1_part2
   WHERE dispatch_date >= '2023-01-01' AND dispatch_date < '2025-01-01'
     AND text_general_code IN ('Aggravated Assault Firearm',

                               'Robbery Firearm', 'Robbery No Firearm')"
) %>%
  filter(!st_is_empty(.)) %>%

```

Violent crime is defined here as aggravated assaults and robberies, with or without a firearm, in 2023 and 2024.

### Build two features

```r
#| eval: false
sales <- sales %>%
  mutate(
    violent_660ft = lengths(st_intersects(st_buffer(., 660), violent)),
    violent_knn5  = sapply(
      st_nn(., violent, k = 5, returnDist = TRUE, progress = FALSE)$dist,
      mean
    )
  )
```

| Feature | How it is built | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `violent_660ft` | Draw a 660 ft buffer around each house, then count crimes that intersect it | **How many** violent crimes are within 1/8 mile |
| `violent_knn5` | Find the 5 nearest crimes to each house, then average their distances | **How close** violent crime is |

How the code works:

- `st_intersects()` returns, for each buffer, the list of crimes inside it. `lengths()` counts them.
- `st_nn()` (from **nngeo**) finds the *k* nearest crimes. With `returnDist = TRUE`, it also returns the distances. `sapply(..., mean)` averages them for each house.

### Which is better?

| | Buffer count | kNN distance |
|---|---|---|
| Can be zero? | Yes, for many houses | Never |
| Direction | More crime = higher value | Smaller distance = more crime nearby |

> **Try both, and let the cross-validated RMSE decide.**

## 11. From "By Eye" to a Number

In the **Week 5 lab** and the **gap assignment**, we compared residual maps **before and after** adding features, by eye.

Today we put **a number** on the question:

> **Do the errors cluster?**

### Tobler's First Law of Geography

> **"Near things are more related than distant things."**

Applied to model errors:

> **If near houses have related errors, the model is missing something about place.**

When errors are random in space, the model has captured location well. When red errors sit together and blue errors sit together, something about those places is not in the model.

---

## 12. The Spatial Lag: My Neighbors' Average

```r
#| eval: false
coords <- st_coordinates(sales)

w_knn <- nb2listw(
  knn2nb(knearneigh(coords, k = 5)),
  style = "W"
)

sales$resid_lag <- lag.listw(w_knn, sales$resid)
```

Step by step (all functions from **spdep**):

| Step | Function | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `st_coordinates()` | Get the x, y coordinates of each house |
| 2 | `knearneigh(coords, k = 5)` | Find each house's 5 nearest houses |
| 3 | `knn2nb()` | Turn that into a **neighbor list** |
| 4 | `nb2listw(..., style = "W")` | Turn neighbors into **weights**. `"W"` = row-standardized: each neighbor gets weight 1/5 |
| 5 | `lag.listw()` | For each house, compute the weighted average of its neighbors' residuals |

> **Each house gets the average residual of its 5 nearest neighbors. This is the "spatial lag".**

## 14. Moran's I: The "Scary" Formula

$$
I = \frac{n}{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij}} \cdot
\frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij}(x_i - \bar{x})(x_j - \bar{x})}
     {\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}
$$

where:

- $n$ = number of observations
- $x_i$ = the value (here, the residual) at location $i$
- $\bar{x}$ = the mean value
- $w_{ij}$ = the weight between $i$ and $j$ (non-zero if they are neighbors)

### What it is really asking

> **When I'm above average, are my neighbors above average too?**

Breaking down the parts:

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $(x_i - \bar{x})(x_j - \bar{x})$ | Positive if neighbors are on the **same side** of the mean, negative if they differ |
| $\sum \sum w_{ij}(\dots)$ | Add this up over all **neighbor pairs** |
| $\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2$ | Divide by the total variation, to scale it |
| $n / \sum \sum w_{ij}$ | Adjust for the number of observations and links |

---

## 15. Worked Example: Five Houses on a Block

Five houses sit in a row. Each house's neighbors are the houses **next door**.

```text
A — B — C — D — E
```

### Step 1: Deviations from the mean

Mean residual:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{100 + 80 + 20 - 50 - 70}{5} = \frac{80}{5} = +16\text{k}
$$

| House | Residual | − mean (16k) | Deviation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | +100k | − 16k | **+84** |
| B | +80k | − 16k | **+64** |
| C | +20k | − 16k | **+4** |
| D | −50k | − 16k | **−66** |
| E | −70k | − 16k | **−86** |

A positive residual means we **under-predicted** that house.

### Step 2: Multiply each neighbor pair

| Pair | Product | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| A–B | (+84)(+64) = **+5,376** | Both under-predicted |
| B–C | (+64)(+4) = **+256** | |
| C–D | (+4)(−66) = **−264** | Different signs |
| D–E | (−66)(−86) = **+5,676** | Both over-predicted |
| **Sum** | **11,044** | |

Each pair counts **twice** (A→B and B→A):

$$
2 \times 11{,}044 = 22{,}088
$$

### Step 3: Total variation

$$
\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 7{,}056 + 4{,}096 + 16 + 4{,}356 + 7{,}396 = 22{,}920
$$

### Step 4: Put it together

- $n = 5$ houses
- $\sum \sum w_{ij} = 8$ (4 pairs × 2 directions, each with weight 1)

$$
I = \frac{5}{8} \cdot \frac{22{,}088}{22{,}920} \approx \mathbf{0.60}
$$

> **Strong clustering: the under-predictions sit together, and so do the over-predictions.**

---

## 16. Reading Moran's I

| Value of I | Pattern | For our errors |
|---|---|---|
| Near **+1** | Similar values cluster | The model is **missing something about place** |
| Near **0** | No spatial pattern | The errors look like **noise in space** |
| **Below 0** | Neighbors differ | Rare for errors. **Check your weights** |

The goal is to push Moran's I of the residuals **toward 0** as you add features.

---

## 17. Is That Big? Shuffle and See

```r
#| eval: false
mc <- moran.mc(sales$resid, w_knn, nsim = 499)

mc$statistic
mc$p.value
```

```text
statistic
0.2605251

[1] 0.002
```

### How the permutation test works

```text
Compute the real Moran's I
        ↓
Shuffle the residuals randomly across houses (499 times)
        ↓
Compute Moran's I for every shuffle
        ↓
Does the real I beat almost all the shuffles?
        ↓
If yes → the clustering isn't luck
```

With 499 shuffles, the **smallest possible p-value** is:

$$
\frac{1}{499 + 1} = 0.002
$$

So $p = 0.002$ means the real I was larger than **every** shuffle.

Here, **I ≈ 0.26**: moderate clustering of errors that is clearly not random.

## 18. p-Value Warning, Again

With about 20,000 houses, **nearly everything is "significant"**.

So the p-value tells us very little.

> **Watch the size of I fall as you add features. That's the diagnostic.**

## 19. Clustered Errors Are a Feature You Haven't Built Yet

If Moran's I is high, ask:

> **What's true about those places that the model doesn't know?**

| What's missing | Fix |
|---|---|
| An amenity or disamenity you haven't measured (parks, transit, crime, vacancy) | Build a **spatial feature** |
| Something about the whole neighborhood | Add a **neighborhood fixed effect** |
| A relationship that differs by place | Add an **interaction** |

Then:

```text
Refit the model
        ↓
Recompute CV RMSE and Moran's I
        ↓
Log both numbers
```

## 20. "My Spatial Stats Class Says Use a Spatial Lag Model"

A **spatial lag model** puts neighbors' **outcomes** on the right-hand side:

$$
y = \rho W y + X\beta + \varepsilon
$$

where $Wy$ is the average price of neighboring houses and $\rho$ measures how strongly it matters.

That is the right tool for **inference about spillovers** (for example, "does a house's price depend on its neighbors' prices?").

For **prediction**, it fails in three ways:

1. **It needs neighbors' prices.** To price a house, you would need the prices of nearby houses that have **not sold yet**.
2. **It leaks.** In cross-validation, a test house's neighbors in the training set **carry its answer**.
3. **It explains nothing.** "It's near expensive houses" is **not a reason** a policymaker can act on.

### Our rule

> **Spatial features of X, never Y.**

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| Crime near the house | Prices near the house |
| Distance to transit, parks, City Hall | Neighbors' sale prices |
