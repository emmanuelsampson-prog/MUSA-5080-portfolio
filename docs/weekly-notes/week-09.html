Week 9: Predictive Policing: Counts, Fishnets, and Dirty Data

Should we build predictive policing?
Published

October 30, 2026

1. Overview

Lecturer: Dr. Elizabeth Delmelle

Lecture title: Predictive Policing. Week 9: Counts, Fishnets, and Dirty Data (not dirty dancing)

This week has two threads, and the lecture insists we need both:

Technical questions Critical questions
How do we model crime counts? Whose data? Whose crimes?
What spatial features predict crime? What if the data is “dirty”?
How do we validate predictions? Who benefits? Who is harmed?
Can we outperform baseline methods? What feedback loops are created?
Can technical solutions fix social problems?

We learn the technical methods AND critically interrogate their use. Both skills are essential for ethical data science.

The opening video (watch here) asks one question to keep in mind all session:

If a model learns from police records, what is it actually learning?

2. Opening Thought: Should We?

Before discussing how to build predictive policing systems, we need to ask whether we should.

“A statistically ‘good’ model can still be socially harmful.”

This continues the thread from last week’s RealPage case:

  • What’s in the data, and who put it there?
  • Who acts on the prediction, and can they say no?
  • Who pays for the errors, and for the “right” answers?

This week the data are police records, the people acting on predictions are patrol commanders, and those who pay are residents of patrolled blocks.

3. The Sales Pitch

What vendors and police departments claim

Claim Promise
Efficiency “Deploy limited resources where they’re needed most”
Objectivity “Remove human bias from decision-making”
Proactivity “Prevent crime before it happens”
Data-driven “Let the data tell us where crime will occur”

Sounds great. But these claims rest on four critical assumptions:

  1. That crime data accurately reflects crime (it doesn’t)
  2. That past patterns predict future crime (they might just predict policing)
  3. That we can separate “good” from “bad” data (we often can’t)
  4. That technical solutions can fix social problems (they can’t)

4. The Technical Evolution

Generation Method Data used Example
1st: Hotspots Kernel density Past crime locations KDE maps
2nd: Risk terrain Logistic regression Crime + features RTM software
3rd: Machine learning Random forest, neural nets Everything PredPol, Palantir
4th: Person-based Network analysis Social connections Chicago’s Strategic Subject List

Each generation claims to be more “objective” and “accurate”.

But what if they’re all built on the same flawed foundation?

5. What Is “Dirty Data”?

Traditional definition (data mining)

  • Missing data
  • Incorrect data
  • Non-standardized formats

Extended definition (Richardson, Schultz & Crawford, 2019)

“Data derived from or influenced by corrupt, biased, and unlawful practices, including data that has been intentionally manipulated or ‘juked,’ as well as data that is distorted by individual and societal biases.”

The authors studied 13 jurisdictions that used or built predictive policing while under federal investigation or court-monitored reform (consent decrees). Their case studies were Chicago, New Orleans and Maricopa County, Arizona.

6. The Many Forms of Dirty Data

Form Examples
1. Fabricated / manipulated False arrests (planted evidence). Crimes downgraded to “juke the stats”. Victims pressured not to file reports
2. Systematically biased Over-policing of certain communities → more recorded “crime”. Under-policing of white-collar crime → it appears less common. Racial profiling → disproportionate stops and arrests
3. Missing / incomplete Unreported crimes (especially in over-policed areas with low trust in police). Ignored complaints. Incomplete records
4. Proxy problems Arrests ≠ crimes committed. Calls for service ≠ actual need. Gang database ≠ actual gang membership

To the model, it’s all just Y. It can’t tell a real burglary from a record produced by a bad practice.

7. The Feedback Loop

The confirmation bias loop (played as a game in class):

Algorithm learns: "Crime happens in neighborhood X"
        ↓
Police are sent to neighborhood X
        ↓
More arrests in neighborhood X (regardless of actual crime)
        ↓
Algorithm "confirmed": "We were right about neighborhood X!"
        ↓
Cycle intensifies → back to the top

The model’s predictions change where records get created, and those records become the next round of training data.

8. The Modeling Workflow

Step What we do
1. Setup & data Load 2024 burglaries (points) and 2024 311 calls. Create a fishnet (500 ft × 500 ft grid). Aggregate burglaries to cells
2. Baseline Kernel density estimation (KDE): simple spatial smoothing. This is what we need to beat
3. Feature engineering Disorder indicators from 311: count in cell, nearest and kNN distances, LISA hotspots, distance to hotspots
4. Count regression Fit Poisson regression. Test for overdispersion. Fit negative binomial if needed. Interpret coefficients
5. Spatial CV Leave-one-group-out (LOGO): train on n − 1 police districts, test on the held-out one. Calculate MAE/RMSE (next week)
6. Comparison Compare with the KDE baseline on 2025 burglaries. Map predictions vs. actual. Analyze errors spatially

Steps 4 (negative binomial) and 5 (geographic CV) start properly next week.

10. What’s a Row?

In the house price assignment, every row was a house.

A burglary is a point. So what is a row here?

We need a unit of analysis to count burglaries in.

Why not census tracts?

  • Tracts vary hugely in size, so their counts aren’t comparable
  • Tract boundaries were drawn for population, not crime
  • MAUP (the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem): move the boundaries and the pattern moves. Remember the Week 4 boundary-change join?

11. The Fishnet: Lay a Grid Over the City

A fishnet is a regular grid of equal-sized cells. Every cell is the same size, so counts are comparable.

#| eval: false
fishnet <- st_make_grid(philly, cellsize = 500, square = TRUE) %>%
  st_sf() %>%
  st_filter(philly) %>%                 # keep cells that touch the city
  mutate(cell_id = row_number())

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(burglaries = lengths(st_intersects(., burg_2025)))

nrow(fishnet)
[1] 16414
Code Meaning
st_make_grid(philly, cellsize = 500) A grid of 500 × 500 squares over Philadelphia’s bounding box
st_sf() Turn the grid into an sf data frame
st_filter(philly) Keep only cells that touch the city
lengths(st_intersects(., burg_2025)) Count 2025 burglaries in each cell
  • cellsize = 500 means 500 feet, because we are in EPSG 2272.
  • What would 500 mean in EPSG 4326? 500 degrees: one cell bigger than the whole Earth.
  • lengths(st_intersects()) is a point-in-polygon count, the same as your buffer counts.

Result: 16,414 cells. Each cell is now a row.

13. Three Reasons OLS Breaks

These are ŷ problems (problems with predictions):

  1. It predicts negative burglaries. A straight line through a pile of zeros dips below zero.
  2. The spread grows with the mean. Busy cells vary a lot and quiet cells barely vary, so the busy cells dominate RMSE.
  3. Counts are whole numbers ≥ 0. OLS doesn’t know that.

Can we just log Y?

Previously we logged skewed predictors and kept Y in dollars. Here:

\[ \log(0) \text{ is undefined} \]

and most cells are zero.

log(y + 1) is a trick, but the +1 is arbitrary, and it changes the answer.

14. Poisson Regression: Log the Mean, Not the Data

\[ Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \]

\[ \log(\lambda) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots \]

\[ \lambda = e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots} \quad \text{always} > 0 \]

where \(\lambda\) is the expected count in a cell.

  • The linear part is the one you already know
  • The exp() keeps predictions positive
  • We log the expected value, not the data, so zeros are no problem

Reading a coefficient

exp(β) is the multiplicative change in the expected count for a one-unit change in x.

For example:

\[ e^{0.20} \approx 1.22 \]

Each one-unit increase in x means 22% more expected burglaries.

exp(β) Meaning
> 1 More expected burglaries: (exp(β) − 1) × 100% more
= 1 No effect
< 1 Fewer expected burglaries: (1 − exp(β)) × 100% fewer

15. One Property to Check: Mean = Variance

A Poisson model assumes the variance equals the mean.

#| eval: false
c(mean = mean(fishnet$burglaries), variance = var(fishnet$burglaries))
     mean  variance
0.1827099 0.3379670

The variance (0.34) is almost twice the mean (0.18).

  • A variance much bigger than the mean is called overdispersion
  • The fix is negative binomial regression (MASS::glm.nb()): the same idea, plus one extra parameter for the spread
  • We use it next week. Today, just know what the check looks like.

17. Building the Features

#| eval: false
centroids <- st_centroid(fishnet)

knn_dist <- function(from, to, k) {
  nn <- get.knnx(st_coordinates(to), st_coordinates(from), k = k)
  rowMeans(nn$nn.dist)
}

city_hall <- st_sfc(st_point(c(-75.1635, 39.9526)), crs = 4326) %>%
  st_transform(2272)

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(
    cars_cnt         = lengths(st_intersects(., abandoned_cars)),  # count in cell
    light_nn1        = knn_dist(centroids, light_outages, k = 1),  # nearest distance
    graffiti_nn3     = knn_dist(centroids, graffiti, k = 3),       # kNN distance
    cityhall_mi      = as.numeric(st_distance(centroids, city_hall)) / 5280,
    log_light_nn1    = log(light_nn1),                             # log the distances
    log_graffiti_nn3 = log(graffiti_nn3)
  )
Feature Type Meaning
cars_cnt Count in cell Abandoned-vehicle calls in the cell
light_nn1 Nearest distance Feet from the cell centre to the nearest streetlight outage
graffiti_nn3 kNN distance Mean feet to the 3 nearest graffiti reports
cityhall_mi Distance to fixed point Miles to City Hall
log_... Logged distance Close changes matter more than far ones

How knn_dist() works

  • get.knnx() (from FNN) finds, for each cell centroid, the k nearest points and their distances
  • rowMeans(nn$nn.dist) averages those k distances
  • With k = 1, it is simply the nearest distance

Distances are measured from each cell’s centroid (its centre point).

All four feature types come from 2024. The outcome is 2025.

18. New Feature: The Hotspot

Last week’s LISA, now run on burglaries instead of residuals:

#| eval: false
fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(burg_2024 = lengths(st_intersects(., burg_2024)))

nb <- knn2nb(knearneigh(st_coordinates(centroids), k = 8))  # 8 neighbors ≈ queen on a grid
lw <- nb2listw(nb, style = "W")

set.seed(5080)
lisa <- localmoran_perm(fishnet$burg_2024, lw, nsim = 999)

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(
    quadrant = attr(lisa, "quadr")$mean,
    p_value  = lisa[, "Pr(folded) Sim"],
    hotspot  = quadrant == "High-High" & p_value < 0.05
  )

table(fishnet$hotspot)
FALSE  TRUE
15639   775
Step Meaning
burg_2024 Count of 2024 burglaries per cell
k = 8 8 nearest cells ≈ queen contiguity on a grid (the 8 surrounding squares)
localmoran_perm(..., nsim = 999) Local Moran’s I with 999 permutations
attr(lisa, "quadr")$mean Each cell’s quadrant (High–High, Low–Low…)
hotspot High–High and p < 0.05

Result: 775 hotspot cells.

A warning

With this many cells, some “hotspots” are there by chance alone.

At p < 0.05 across 16,414 tests, about 5% (roughly 820) could come up significant with no real pattern at all, in some quadrant. Last week’s LISA used a stricter α = 0.01 for exactly this reason.

19. Turn the Hotspot Into a Feature, and Check for Leakage

#| eval: false
hot_pts <- centroids[fishnet$hotspot, ]

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(hotspot_dist_mi = knn_dist(centroids, hot_pts, k = 1) / 5280)

hotspot_dist_mi = miles from each cell to the nearest hotspot cell.

The leakage check

Would I have this number at the moment of prediction?

Hotspots built from Used to predict Leakage?
2025 burglaries 2025 burglaries Yes. That’s leakage: the feature contains the answer
2024 burglaries 2025 burglaries No. Train on earlier, predict later

This is why we loaded two years of data.

20. OLS vs. Poisson, Same Features

#| eval: false
f <- burglaries ~ cars_cnt + log_light_nn1 + log_graffiti_nn3 +
                  cityhall_mi + hotspot_dist_mi

ols  <- lm(f, data = fishnet)
pois <- glm(f, family = "poisson", data = fishnet)

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(pred_ols  = predict(ols),
         pred_pois = predict(pois, type = "response"))  # counts, not log-counts!

c(negative_ols = sum(fishnet$pred_ols < 0),
  negative_pois = sum(fishnet$pred_pois < 0))
 negative_ols negative_pois
         3277             0
  • OLS predicts negative burglaries in 3,277 cells (about 20% of the city)
  • Poisson predicts none

Important: type = "response"

Without it, predict() on a Poisson model returns log-counts, and your RMSE is nonsense.

predict() call Returns
predict(pois) log(λ): log expected counts
predict(pois, type = "response") λ: expected counts

Template from class

*“Each additional ___ means ___% more/fewer expected burglaries.”*

A note on logged distances

For a logged predictor, “one unit” means multiplying the distance by e (about 2.7). It is easier to talk about doubling: multiply the expected count by \(2^{\beta}\).

The strongest feature by far is distance to last year’s hotspots: burglary is very persistent in space.

23. The Traditional Hotspot Map: Kernel Density

Before any model, departments drew a smoothed map of last year’s crime (kernel density) and patrolled the hot parts.

That’s the bar our model has to clear.

#| eval: false
bb  <- st_bbox(fishnet)  # grid extent: every centroid falls inside the window
win <- owin(xrange = c(bb["xmin"], bb["xmax"]),
            yrange = c(bb["ymin"], bb["ymax"]))

xy_2024  <- st_coordinates(st_filter(burg_2024, philly))       # drop bad geocodes
kde_2024 <- density(ppp(xy_2024[, 1], xy_2024[, 2], window = win),
                    sigma = 1000)                              # 1000 ft bandwidth

xy_cent <- st_coordinates(centroids)
fishnet$kde_2024 <- safelookup(kde_2024,
                               ppp(xy_cent[, 1], xy_cent[, 2], window = win))
Step (spatstat) Meaning
owin() The study window (the grid’s rectangle)
ppp() A point pattern object from 2024 burglary coordinates
density(..., sigma = 1000) Kernel density surface with a 1,000 ft bandwidth
safelookup() Read the density value at each cell centroid

In this run, last year’s smoothed map captures more of 2025’s burglaries than the model.

This test is rigged in the model’s favour

  • The model was fit on 2025 (its outcome is 2025 burglaries)
  • The KDE has never seen 2025

The lecture asks: if the model only barely wins with that head start, what does that tell you? Here it does not even win.

A fair version holds data out. That is where we start next week.

The question the reading ends on: biased, but useful?

If the model can’t clearly beat last year’s map, what are we getting in exchange for its complexity and its bias?

25. Where Does a Burglary Record Come From?

Add a box in front of “outcome”:

something happens → someone reports it → police record it → "outcome" → … → patrol decision
                                                                                │
                         ◄─────────────── more police here ─────────────────────┘

\[ \text{recorded} = \text{actual} \times P(\text{reported}) \times P(\text{recorded} \mid \text{reported}) \]

Term Who decides?
actual What really happened
P(reported) A human decision: does the victim or a witness call?
P(recorded | reported) A human decision: does the officer write it up, and as what?
  • In the house price assignment: a sale price is recorded when the house sells. Nearly every sale is in the data.
  • Here: two of the three terms are human decisions, and both vary by place.

For each crime type:

  2. Drug possession
  3. Theft (a package stolen from a porch)

Answer:

  • Who creates the record? The victim calling it in, or an officer observing it?
  • What would make it show up more in one neighborhood than another, even if it happens equally often?
  • Would you build a patrol model on it? Yes / no / only if…

Possible answers

27. How Much Actually Gets Reported?

Share of victimizations reported to police (National Crime Victimization Survey, 2024):

Crime Reported to police
Motor vehicle theft ~75%
Burglary / trespassing ~41%
Other theft ~25%
  • Drug possession is almost entirely officer-generated. More patrol means more records, and the loop closes on itself.
  • That’s why demos like ours use burglary: it is victim-reported, so it is less patrol-driven.
  • But even burglary is reported less than half the time.
  • According to the lecture, urban residents report a smaller share of property crime than suburban or rural residents. Trust in police is part of P(reported).

28. Dirty Data, Bad Predictions

Richardson, Schultz & Crawford (2019):

  • Predictive systems were trained on data produced during periods of documented flawed, racially biased and sometimes unlawful policing
  • They examined jurisdictions that used or built predictive policing while under federal investigation or consent decrees. Case studies: Chicago, New Orleans, Maricopa County
  • “Dirty” means more than biased patrol. It includes manipulated records: downgraded crimes, planted evidence, stops that should never have happened

The model can’t tell a real burglary from a record produced by a bad practice. To the model, it’s all just Y.

29. Why Cross-Validation Can’t Save You

Cross-validation can’t catch bias that’s in the test set too.

House price assignment Predictive policing
Held-out fold contains… The true sale price The same recorded data
When the model is wrong… The test fold tells you, and you find residual clusters The test fold may agree with the bias

If the records over-count Neighborhood A:

Model predicts high in A → test fold (also over-counted) agrees → RMSE looks great

A low RMSE means you predicted the records well, not the crime.

The bias goes in twice

The hotspot feature is built from the outcome (2024 burglary records). So biased records enter:

  1. Once as Y
  2. Once as a feature

30. The Features Are a Theory, Too

Look at what we chose to predict burglary with:

Feature What it measures
Abandoned cars Disorder
Streetlights out Disorder
Graffiti Disorder
  • This is broken windows theory: visible disorder signals that no one is watching, which invites crime
  • In practice it became broken windows policing: aggressive enforcement of minor offences in disordered places
  • A model built on disorder features sends patrols to disordered places, whether or not the theory is right

And those features are 311 calls: someone decided to report the graffiti.

Who calls 311?

The bias isn’t only in the data. It’s in which features we chose.

31. Table Question (5 min)

In the house price assignment, errors that cluster in places land on people: over-predicted means over-taxed.

Here, who lands in an over-predicted cell, and what happens to them that wouldn’t happen in the house price assignment?

Points to consider:

  • An over-predicted cell gets more patrols
  • More patrols mean more stops, searches and arrests for residents, including for minor offences
  • Those encounters create new records, which feed the next model (the feedback loop)
  • An over-taxed homeowner loses money. An over-patrolled resident can lose liberty, gain a criminal record, or face a dangerous encounter

34. Key Concepts to Remember

  • A statistically “good” model can still be socially harmful. Ask should we before how.
  • Predictive policing rests on four shaky assumptions: that crime data reflect crime, that past patterns predict crime rather than policing, that good and bad data can be separated, and that technical fixes solve social problems.
  • Dirty data (Richardson, Schultz & Crawford, 2019) includes data shaped by corrupt, biased or unlawful practices: fabricated, biased, missing and proxy data.
  • Arrests ≠ crimes. Calls for service ≠ need. Gang database ≠ gang membership.
  • The feedback loop: predictions send police, police create records, records “confirm” the predictions.
  • A fishnet gives equal-sized cells, so counts are comparable and the MAUP problem of tracts is reduced.
  • Check your CRS: cellsize = 500 is feet in EPSG 2272 but degrees in EPSG 4326.
  • Crime counts are mostly zeros (about 87% of cells here).
  • OLS breaks for counts: negative predictions, spread that grows with the mean, and no respect for whole numbers.
  • log(y + 1) is an arbitrary trick. Poisson regression logs the mean, not the data.
  • In Poisson, exp(β) is the multiplicative change in the expected count.
  • Use predict(..., type = "response") to get counts, not log-counts.
  • Poisson assumes mean = variance. A larger variance is overdispersion; use negative binomial (next week).
  • Four feature types: count in cell, nearest distance, kNN distance, distance to a fixed point.
  • A LISA hotspot feature uses last year’s High–High clusters. With thousands of cells, some hotspots appear by chance.
  • Leakage check: would I have this number at the moment of prediction? Build features from 2024, predict 2025.
  • The bar to beat is the kernel density map of last year’s crime. In this run it caught 46% of 2025 burglaries in its top 10% of cells, against the model’s 40%, even though the test favoured the model.
  • Recorded = actual × P(reported) × P(recorded | reported). Two of the three terms are human decisions that vary by place.
  • Burglary is victim-reported (but only ~41% of the time). Drug possession is officer-generated, so it measures policing.
  • Cross-validation can’t catch bias that’s in the test set too. A low RMSE means you predicted the records well, not the crime.
  • The bias can enter twice: as Y and as a feature built from Y.
  • Disorder features encode broken windows theory, and 311 calls depend on who calls.
  • Over-predicted cells mean more patrols for the people who live there, not just higher tax bills.
  • Biased outcome measurement hurts both β̂ and ŷ, and CV won’t show it.

## 5. What Is "Dirty Data"?

### Traditional definition (data mining)

- Missing data
- Incorrect data
- Non-standardized formats

### Extended definition (Richardson, Schultz & Crawford, 2019)

> **"Data derived from or influenced by corrupt, biased, and unlawful practices, including data that has been intentionally manipulated or 'juked,' as well as data that is distorted by individual and societal biases."**

The authors studied **13 jurisdictions** that used or built predictive policing **while** under federal investigation or court-monitored reform (consent decrees). Their case studies were **Chicago**, **New Orleans** and **Maricopa County, Arizona**.

---

## 6. The Many Forms of Dirty Data

| Form | Examples |
|---|---|
| **1. Fabricated / manipulated** | False arrests (planted evidence). Crimes downgraded to "juke the stats". Victims pressured not to file reports |
| **2. Systematically biased** | Over-policing of certain communities → more recorded "crime". Under-policing of white-collar crime → it appears less common. Racial profiling → disproportionate stops and arrests |
| **3. Missing / incomplete** | Unreported crimes (especially in over-policed areas with low trust in police). Ignored complaints. Incomplete records |
| **4. Proxy problems** | Arrests ≠ crimes committed. Calls for service ≠ actual need. Gang database ≠ actual gang membership |

> **To the model, it's all just Y. It can't tell a real burglary from a record produced by a bad practice.**

---

## 7. The Feedback Loop

The **confirmation bias loop** (played as a game in class):

```text
Algorithm learns: "Crime happens in neighborhood X"
        ↓
Police are sent to neighborhood X
        ↓
More arrests in neighborhood X (regardless of actual crime)
        ↓
Algorithm "confirmed": "We were right about neighborhood X!"
        ↓
Cycle intensifies → back to the top
```

The model's predictions change **where records get created**, and those records become the next round of training data.

## 8. The Modeling Workflow

| Step | What we do |
|---|---|
| **1. Setup & data** | Load **2024** burglaries (points) and **2024** 311 calls. Create a **fishnet** (500 ft × 500 ft grid). Aggregate burglaries to cells |
| **2. Baseline** | **Kernel density estimation (KDE)**: simple spatial smoothing. This is what we need to beat |
| **3. Feature engineering** | Disorder indicators from 311: **count** in cell, **nearest** and **kNN** distances, **LISA** hotspots, **distance to hotspots** |
| **4. Count regression** | Fit **Poisson** regression. Test for **overdispersion**. Fit **negative binomial** if needed. Interpret coefficients |
| **5. Spatial CV** | **Leave-one-group-out (LOGO)**: train on n − 1 police districts, test on the held-out one. Calculate MAE/RMSE (**next week**) |
| **6. Comparison** | Compare with the KDE baseline on **2025** burglaries. Map predictions vs. actual. Analyze errors spatially |

Steps 4 (negative binomial) and 5 (geographic CV) start properly **next week**.

## 10. What's a Row?

In the house price assignment, **every row was a house**.

A burglary is a **point**. So what is a row here?

We need a **unit of analysis** to count burglaries in.

### Why not census tracts?

- Tracts vary hugely in **size**, so their counts aren't comparable
- Tract boundaries were drawn for **population**, not crime
- **MAUP** (the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem): move the boundaries and the pattern moves. Remember the Week 4 boundary-change join?

## 11. The Fishnet: Lay a Grid Over the City

A **fishnet** is a regular grid of equal-sized cells. Every cell is the same size, so counts are comparable.

```r

  st_sf() %>%
  st_filter(philly) %>%                 # keep cells that touch the city
  mutate(cell_id = row_number())

fishnet <- fishnet %>%

```

```text

```

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `st_make_grid(philly, cellsize = 500)` | A grid of 500 × 500 squares over Philadelphia's bounding box |
| `st_sf()` | Turn the grid into an `sf` data frame |
| `st_filter(philly)` | Keep only cells that touch the city |
| `lengths(st_intersects(., burg_2025))` | Count 2025 burglaries in each cell |

## 13. Three Reasons OLS Breaks

These are **ŷ problems** (problems with predictions):

1. **It predicts negative burglaries.** A straight line through a pile of zeros dips below zero.
2. **The spread grows with the mean.** Busy cells vary a lot and quiet cells barely vary, so the busy cells dominate RMSE.
3. **Counts are whole numbers ≥ 0.** OLS doesn't know that.

### Can we just log Y?

Previously we logged skewed **predictors** and kept Y in dollars. Here:

$$
\log(0) \text{ is undefined}
$$

and most cells are zero.

`log(y + 1)` is a trick, but the **+1 is arbitrary, and it changes the answer**.

---

## 14. Poisson Regression: Log the Mean, Not the Data

$$
Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)
$$

$$
\log(\lambda) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots
$$

$$
\lambda = e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots} \quad \text{always} > 0
$$

where $\lambda$ is the **expected count** in a cell.

- The **linear part** is the one you already know
- The **exp()** keeps predictions positive
- We log the **expected value**, not the data, so zeros are no problem

### Reading a coefficient

> **exp(β) is the multiplicative change in the expected count for a one-unit change in x.**

For example:

$$
e^{0.20} \approx 1.22
$$

Each one-unit increase in x means **22% more** expected burglaries.

| exp(β) | Meaning |
|---|---|
| > 1 | More expected burglaries: (exp(β) − 1) × 100% more |
| = 1 | No effect |
| < 1 | Fewer expected burglaries: (1 − exp(β)) × 100% fewer |

---

## 15. One Property to Check: Mean = Variance

A Poisson model assumes the **variance equals the mean**.

```r
#| eval: false
c(mean = mean(fishnet$burglaries), variance = var(fishnet$burglaries))
```

```text
     mean  variance
0.1827099 0.3379670
```

The variance (0.34) is almost **twice** the mean (0.18).

- A variance much bigger than the mean is called **overdispersion**
- The fix is **negative binomial regression** (`MASS::glm.nb()`): the same idea, plus one extra parameter for the spread
- We use it **next week**. Today, just know what the check looks like.

## 17. Building the Features

```r
#| eval: false
centroids <- st_centroid(fishnet)

knn_dist <- function(from, to, k) {
  nn <- get.knnx(st_coordinates(to), st_coordinates(from), k = k)
  rowMeans(nn$nn.dist)
}

city_hall <- st_sfc(st_point(c(-75.1635, 39.9526)), crs = 4326) %>%
  st_transform(2272)

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(
    cars_cnt         = lengths(st_intersects(., abandoned_cars)),  # count in cell
    light_nn1        = knn_dist(centroids, light_outages, k = 1),  # nearest distance
    graffiti_nn3     = knn_dist(centroids, graffiti, k = 3),       # kNN distance
    cityhall_mi      = as.numeric(st_distance(centroids, city_hall)) / 5280,
    log_light_nn1    = log(light_nn1),                             # log the distances
    log_graffiti_nn3 = log(graffiti_nn3)
  )
```

| Feature | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `cars_cnt` | Count in cell | Abandoned-vehicle calls in the cell |
| `light_nn1` | Nearest distance | Feet from the cell centre to the nearest streetlight outage |
| `graffiti_nn3` | kNN distance | Mean feet to the 3 nearest graffiti reports |
| `cityhall_mi` | Distance to fixed point | Miles to City Hall |
| `log_...` | Logged distance | Close changes matter more than far ones |

### How `knn_dist()` works

- `get.knnx()` (from **FNN**) finds, for each cell centroid, the *k* nearest points and their distances
- `rowMeans(nn$nn.dist)` averages those *k* distances
- With `k = 1`, it is simply the **nearest distance**

Distances are measured from each cell's **centroid** (its centre point).

> **All four feature types come from 2024. The outcome is 2025.**

## 18. New Feature: The Hotspot

Last week's **LISA**, now run on **burglaries** instead of residuals:

```r
#| eval: false
fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(burg_2024 = lengths(st_intersects(., burg_2024)))

nb <- knn2nb(knearneigh(st_coordinates(centroids), k = 8))  # 8 neighbors ≈ queen on a grid
lw <- nb2listw(nb, style = "W")

set.seed(5080)
lisa <- localmoran_perm(fishnet$burg_2024, lw, nsim = 999)

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(
    quadrant = attr(lisa, "quadr")$mean,
    p_value  = lisa[, "Pr(folded) Sim"],
    hotspot  = quadrant == "High-High" & p_value < 0.05
  )

table(fishnet$hotspot)
```

```text
FALSE  TRUE
15639   775
```

| Step | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `burg_2024` | Count of **2024** burglaries per cell |
| `k = 8` | 8 nearest cells ≈ **queen contiguity** on a grid (the 8 surrounding squares) |
| `localmoran_perm(..., nsim = 999)` | Local Moran's I with 999 permutations |
| `attr(lisa, "quadr")$mean` | Each cell's quadrant (High–High, Low–Low…) |
| `hotspot` | **High–High** and **p < 0.05** |

Result: **775 hotspot cells**.

### A warning

> **With this many cells, some "hotspots" are there by chance alone.**

At p < 0.05 across 16,414 tests, about 5% (roughly 820) could come up significant with no real pattern at all, in some quadrant. Last week's LISA used a stricter α = 0.01 for exactly this reason.

## 19. Turn the Hotspot Into a Feature, and Check for Leakage

```r
#| eval: false
hot_pts <- centroids[fishnet$hotspot, ]

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(hotspot_dist_mi = knn_dist(centroids, hot_pts, k = 1) / 5280)
```

`hotspot_dist_mi` = miles from each cell to the **nearest hotspot cell**.

### The leakage check

> **Would I have this number at the moment of prediction?**

| Hotspots built from | Used to predict | Leakage? |
|---|---|---|
| **2025** burglaries | **2025** burglaries | **Yes. That's leakage**: the feature contains the answer |
| **2024** burglaries | **2025** burglaries | **No.** Train on earlier, predict later |

This is why we loaded **two years** of data.

## 20. OLS vs. Poisson, Same Features

```r
#| eval: false
f <- burglaries ~ cars_cnt + log_light_nn1 + log_graffiti_nn3 +
                  cityhall_mi + hotspot_dist_mi

ols  <- lm(f, data = fishnet)
pois <- glm(f, family = "poisson", data = fishnet)

fishnet <- fishnet %>%
  mutate(pred_ols  = predict(ols),
         pred_pois = predict(pois, type = "response"))  # counts, not log-counts!

c(negative_ols = sum(fishnet$pred_ols < 0),
  negative_pois = sum(fishnet$pred_pois < 0))
```

```text
 negative_ols negative_pois
         3277             0
```

- **OLS** predicts **negative burglaries** in **3,277 cells** (about 20% of the city)
- **Poisson** predicts **none**

### Important: `type = "response"`

> **Without it, `predict()` on a Poisson model returns log-counts, and your RMSE is nonsense.**

| `predict()` call | Returns |
|---|---|
| `predict(pois)` | log(λ): log expected counts |
| `predict(pois, type = "response")` | λ: expected **counts** |

> *"Each additional ___ means ___% more/fewer expected burglaries."*

For a **logged** predictor, "one unit" means multiplying the distance by *e* (about 2.7). It is easier to talk about **doubling**: multiply the expected count by $2^{\beta}$.

## 23. The Traditional Hotspot Map: Kernel Density

Before any model, departments drew a **smoothed map of last year's crime** (kernel density) and patrolled the hot parts.

> **That's the bar our model has to clear.**

```r
#| eval: false
bb  <- st_bbox(fishnet)  # grid extent: every centroid falls inside the window
win <- owin(xrange = c(bb["xmin"], bb["xmax"]),
            yrange = c(bb["ymin"], bb["ymax"]))

xy_2024  <- st_coordinates(st_filter(burg_2024, philly))       # drop bad geocodes
kde_2024 <- density(ppp(xy_2024[, 1], xy_2024[, 2], window = win),
                    sigma = 1000)                              # 1000 ft bandwidth

xy_cent <- st_coordinates(centroids)
fishnet$kde_2024 <- safelookup(kde_2024,
                               ppp(xy_cent[, 1], xy_cent[, 2], window = win))
```

| Step (spatstat) | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `owin()` | The study window (the grid's rectangle) |
| `ppp()` | A **point pattern** object from 2024 burglary coordinates |
| `density(..., sigma = 1000)` | Kernel density surface with a **1,000 ft** bandwidth |
| `safelookup()` | Read the density value at each cell centroid |

`st_filter(burg_2024, philly)` drops burglaries geocoded **outside** the city (bad geocodes).

## 25. Where Does a Burglary Record Come From?

Add a box in front of "outcome":

```text
something happens → someone reports it → police record it → "outcome" → … → patrol decision
                                                                                │
                         ◄─────────────── more police here ─────────────────────┘
```

$$
\text{recorded} = \text{actual} \times P(\text{reported}) \times P(\text{recorded} \mid \text{reported})
$$

| Term | Who decides? |
|---|---|
| actual | What really happened |
| P(reported) | A **human decision**: does the victim or a witness call? |
| P(recorded \| reported) | A **human decision**: does the officer write it up, and as what? |

- **In the house price assignment:** a sale price is recorded when the house sells. **Nearly every sale is in the data.**
- **Here:** **two of the three terms are human decisions**, and both **vary by place**.

## 28. Dirty Data, Bad Predictions

Richardson, Schultz & Crawford (2019):

- Predictive systems were trained on data produced during periods of **documented** flawed, racially biased and sometimes **unlawful** policing
- They examined jurisdictions that used or built predictive policing **while** under federal investigation or consent decrees. Case studies: **Chicago, New Orleans, Maricopa County**
- "Dirty" means more than biased patrol. It includes **manipulated records**: downgraded crimes, planted evidence, stops that should never have happened

> **The model can't tell a real burglary from a record produced by a bad practice. To the model, it's all just Y.**

## 29. Why Cross-Validation Can't Save You

> **Cross-validation can't catch bias that's in the test set too.**

| | House price assignment | Predictive policing |
|---|---|---|
| Held-out fold contains… | The **true sale price** | The **same recorded data** |
| When the model is wrong… | The test fold tells you, and you find residual clusters | The test fold may **agree** with the bias |

If the records over-count Neighborhood A:

```text
Model predicts high in A → test fold (also over-counted) agrees → RMSE looks great
```

> **A low RMSE means you predicted the records well, not the crime.**

### The bias goes in twice

The **hotspot feature is built from the outcome** (2024 burglary records). So biased records enter:

1. Once as **Y**
2. Once as a **feature**

## 30. The Features Are a Theory, Too

Look at what we chose to predict burglary with:

| Feature | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Abandoned cars | Disorder |
| Streetlights out | Disorder |
| Graffiti | Disorder |

- This is **broken windows theory**: visible disorder signals that no one is watching, which invites crime
- In practice it became **broken windows policing**: aggressive enforcement of minor offences in disordered places
- A model built on disorder features sends patrols to disordered places, **whether or not the theory is right**

And those features are **311 calls**: someone decided to report the graffiti.

> **Who calls 311?**

> **The bias isn't only in the data. It's in which features we chose.**

## 34. Key Concepts to Remember

- **A statistically "good" model can still be socially harmful.** Ask *should we* before *how*.
- Predictive policing rests on four shaky assumptions: that crime data reflect crime, that past patterns predict crime rather than policing, that good and bad data can be separated, and that technical fixes solve social problems.
- **Dirty data** (Richardson, Schultz & Crawford, 2019) includes data shaped by corrupt, biased or unlawful practices: fabricated, biased, missing and proxy data.
- **Arrests ≠ crimes. Calls for service ≠ need. Gang database ≠ gang membership.**
- The **feedback loop**: predictions send police, police create records, records "confirm" the predictions.
- A **fishnet** gives equal-sized cells, so counts are comparable and the MAUP problem of tracts is reduced.
- Check your CRS: `cellsize = 500` is feet in EPSG 2272 but degrees in EPSG 4326.
- Crime counts are mostly **zeros** (about 87% of cells here).
- **OLS breaks for counts:** negative predictions, spread that grows with the mean, and no respect for whole numbers.
- `log(y + 1)` is an arbitrary trick. **Poisson regression logs the mean, not the data.**
- In Poisson, **exp(β)** is the multiplicative change in the expected count.
- Use `predict(..., type = "response")` to get counts, not log-counts.
- Poisson assumes **mean = variance**. A larger variance is **overdispersion**; use negative binomial (next week).
- Four feature types: **count in cell, nearest distance, kNN distance, distance to a fixed point**.
- A **LISA hotspot** feature uses last year's High–High clusters. With thousands of cells, some hotspots appear by chance.
- **Leakage check:** would I have this number at the moment of prediction? Build features from **2024**, predict **2025**.
- The bar to beat is the **kernel density map of last year's crime**. In this run it caught 46% of 2025 burglaries in its top 10% of cells, against the model's 40%, even though the test favoured the model.
- **Recorded = actual × P(reported) × P(recorded | reported).** Two of the three terms are human decisions that vary by place.
- **Burglary** is victim-reported (but only ~41% of the time). **Drug possession** is officer-generated, so it measures policing.
- **Cross-validation can't catch bias that's in the test set too.** A low RMSE means you predicted the records well, not the crime.
- The bias can enter **twice**: as Y and as a feature built from Y.
- Disorder features encode **broken windows theory**, and 311 calls depend on **who calls**.
- Over-predicted cells mean **more patrols** for the people who live there, not just higher tax bills.
- **Biased outcome measurement hurts both β̂ and ŷ, and CV won't show it.**
